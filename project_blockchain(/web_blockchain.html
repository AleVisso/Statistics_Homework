Native SHA-256 vs. simplified hash

The native crypto.subtle.digest API is compared with an additive checksum (the sum of character codes). The defaults are two texts with swapped digits: identical for the checksum, different for SHA-256.

Mining times vs. probabilistic models

Each experiment performs M real nonce searches with SHA-256 on random inputs. With difficulty d the success probability per attempt is p = 16−d: the number of attempts is Geometric and the waiting time is approximately Exponential.

Note: d = 3 with a large M may take from 1 to 5 minutes depending on the PC, since every attempt is a real asynchronous SHA-256 call.

CV = standard deviation / mean: for a geometric variable with small p it is about 1 (like the exponential). KS D is the maximum distance between the empirical CDF and the theoretical CDF 1−(1−p)n; since the variable is discrete, the test is conservative.

Probability of rewriting already accepted blocks

An attacker with hash-power share q tries to recover a deficit of z confirmations. Three estimates are compared: Nakamoto's Poisson approximation, the exact formula (negative binomial, Rosenfeld) and a Monte Carlo simulation.

P(success) = 1 − Σk=0..z Poisson(k; λ) · [1 − (q/p)z−k],   λ = z·q/p,   p = 1 − q

Last column: minimum confirmations for a risk below 0.1%. For q ≥ 0.5 the probability is 1 for any z.

Where the model stops holding

The estimates assume constant hash rates, exponential block times, zero network latency, no difficulty adjustment, and an attacker who mines privately starting from a deficit of z rather than from an advantage. With latency or frequent forks the attacker starts ahead and more confirmations are needed. The gap between the Nakamoto and exact columns already shows how much the result depends on the chosen approximation.
